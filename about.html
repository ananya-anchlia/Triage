300 simulated people with minor injuries, noon to 4 PM, played twice with the same people and the same other arrivals: once with everyone at their nearest ER, once routed by Triage one person at a time. simulated network

Triage is a working prototype, shown on hypothetical cases in a simulated Triangle network. It tells you where a non-emergency injury can be treated and how long the simulated wait is, and it sends you to the ER or 911 at the first warning sign.

1The problem

Emergency rooms are where most people take an injury, and a visit takes hours.

3The queue model

  • Arrivals. People walk in at random (Poisson), at a rate that depends on the kind of place and the hour: quiet overnight, busiest in the evening.
  • Treatment slots. Each place has a fixed number of rooms with staff. A free slot takes the next person in line. Treatment times are random around an average.
  • Lines. People in treatment are tracked apart from people waiting.
  • Emergencies first. ERs also get emergencies. They go to the front of the line but never interrupt someone already being treated.
  • Your wait starts when you arrive, after the drive, not when you press the button.

The method is real; the numbers are not. They were written down once in sim.js with the reason beside each. The two ER arrival rates were lowered once from the first plan, because the first values gave ERs about three times more work than they could do. They were re-set against the CMS figure in section 1 before any Triage comparison was run, and never tuned afterwards.

8Limitations and next steps

  • The hospital feed is simulated. The 12 facilities, their lines and every wait are invented. With real feeds the same code would run on real lines.
  • The parameters are invented. Arrival rates, treatment times and slots are plausible guesses. Real data would replace every one of them, and could change the results on this page.
  • Drive times are approximate. Straight-line distance × 1.3 at 40 km/h. No roads, no traffic, no transit.
  • The simulated Saturday is harsh on ERs. Nobody gives up and leaves a line, and all 300 extra people arrive in four hours. That is why the nearest-ER waits run to many hours.
  • The checklist is a prototype guardrail. It is based on published ACEP warning signs and has not been reviewed by a clinician. It needs clinical validation before real patients rely on it. A clear checklist doesn't rule out a serious injury.
  • Routing moves the wait, it doesn't remove it. Clinics' other patients wait longer, and some people are treated after closing time. Section 5 shows both.
  • Picks stay on this laptop. A pick changes only the simulation in this browser. A real network would keep the shared lines on a server.
  • Next: one real wait-time feed beside the simulated ones, real drive times, transit and rideshare, cost and insurance.
